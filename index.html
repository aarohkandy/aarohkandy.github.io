How prompt detail changed what people preferred in an AI-generated CAD pilot

Aaroh Kandhare · July 2026

More detail sounds like it should make an AI-generated CAD design better. In our first arena pilot, it did not work that cleanly.

We generated snowmen, wall hooks, and wall planters at five prompt-detail levels — 1, 3, 5, 7, and 10 — then placed the renderable outputs into pairwise battles and collected preferences through a public interface. Level 7 came out first overall, but each product family chose a different winner. The interesting result is not that seven is always best. It is that prompt detail appears to have a useful middle range, and that range changes with the object being designed.

The short version

Across the 230 votes accepted for the main analysis, Level 7 earned a 62.5% raw preference score; the next closest was Level 1 at 55.1%. Correcting for the fact that the five levels faced different opponent mixes did not remove the lead — Level 7 remained first at 62.9% in a family-adjusted paired-comparison model. But snowmen favored Level 7, wall hooks slightly favored Level 3, and wall planters favored Level 10. Moderate detail led the pooled archive; it was not a universal recipe.

94 renderable designs · 245 archived votes · 230 accepted · 3 families · 5 detail levels

What happened

The raw pooled scores were 55.1% (L1), 50.0% (L3), 42.7% (L5), 62.5% (L7), and 40.1% (L10). The shape is a spike, not a smooth curve. More instructions did not steadily improve preference, and the least detailed prompt still finished second. Level 7 also had the easiest opponent mix — yet after adjusting for schedule and family it still ranked first.

The pooled model does not erase the direct loss

Level 7 performed well against Levels 1, 5, and 10, but it lost its direct Level-3 matchup — 43.3% in 15 votes, where the fitted model predicted 63.2%. The family splits make the same point: Level 7 led snowmen (66.7%), Level 3 led wall hooks (66.7%), and Level 10 led wall planters (83.3%). Small subsets, but enough to reject the claim that one amount of detail works best for every CAD task.

What 7/10 might mean

A Level-7 prompt looked like a complete design brief without specifying every implementation choice — form, dimensions, wall thickness, open top, flat back, mounting hole. Later levels added drainage layout, exact placements, fillets, watertightness, print orientation. One reading is that the model got enough structure to understand the object while keeping some freedom to resolve the geometry. Plausible, not proven — wall planters actually favored Level 10. The better rule isn't “always stop at seven.” It's “give the model the constraints that define the product, then be careful about instructions that overcoordinate details the generator may not resolve well.”

What this pilot cannot prove

All archived votes share one browser session, so we can't verify how many people voted or claim the percentages represent a population. The full prompt ladder survives only for wall planters. Failed generations are absent, so this measures which surviving render people preferred, not which level was most likely to produce a usable file. The comparison network is thin. The pilot is strong enough to reveal a pattern worth testing — not strong enough to settle the question.

Bottom line

Level 7 was the most preferred condition in this archive, before and after adjustment. But the stronger conclusion is the useful one: prompt specificity should be treated as a design choice, not a volume knob. The right level depends on what the object needs the model to understand, and which choices the model should still be allowed to make.
